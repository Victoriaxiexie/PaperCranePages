People

The people who bring Japanese literature vividly to life—and make it accessible to readers across the Western world possess extraordinary talent. Authors who weave nuanced stories, translators who carefully bridge cultural nuances, and illustrators who enrich narratives through captivating visuals all play essential roles in connecting us to Japan’s literary traditions.

Authors

Reading has always fueled my creativity and sparked my love for storytelling. It made me curious: who wrote the very first novel and decided to pour their imagination onto the page in the form of prose? The prestigious honor belongs to Japan. In the 11th century, Murasaki Shikibu, a remarkable woman authored The Tale of Genji (Genji Monogatari), widely recognized as the world’s first fiction novel. Following the passing of her husband, she withdrew into a life dedicated to Buddhist study, raising her daughter, and immersing herself in writing. Her masterpiece chronicles the life of the charismatic "Shining Prince," Genji, and continues to captivate readers over a millennium later (Penguin Random House Canada).

Translators

Translators are the quiet heroes of literature. Without them, I never would’ve fallen in love with the words of authors oceans away. They do more than swap one language for another, they carry emotion and nuance across cultures. A good translation makes you forget you’re even reading a translation at all. It feels effortless, like the story was always meant for you. That kind of work takes deep empathy, cultural understanding, and an ear for both languages. Thanks to these storytellers behind the scenes, Japanese literature lives and breathes in the hands of readers around the world.

Illustrators

Book cover illustrators are the first to whisper, “Open me.” They capture the soul of a story in a single image, sometimes bold and striking or sometimes quiet and intricate. In Japanese literature, where atmosphere and emotion often live between the lines, the cover becomes part of the storytelling itself. It draws you in before the first page offering a visual hint of what’s to come.
